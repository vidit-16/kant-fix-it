What the research found

Everything else was held fixed: the agents, how hard they search, the world they share. Only the rule changed.

A price beats a conscience

In a simulated interbank payment network, selfish banks left the system 29% costlier than a planner would. A conscience closed about half of that gap. A plain charge on delay did better than every rule.

Pit a conscience against a price

Charge what you can see, and they hide it

In a shared codebase, a charge on visible coupling taught the agents to conceal it, and costs rose by more than half. A tax on the damage itself, wherever it lands, could not be dodged that way.

Make them stop hiding

The conscience fails when agents are unequal

When one module takes most of the incidents, "what if everyone did this?" measures what an agent would suffer, not what it causes. The exposed agent freezes and the sheltered ones run wild. The damage tax holds.

Try it with one module taking the hits

It holds whatever you call "the same change"

A conscience needs a scale on which to compare changes. On four different scales its result moved a lot, but none of them beat the damage tax.

Predictions that failed

  • FailedBanks of different sizes would weaken the conscience. They did not.
  • FailedOne selfish bank among conscientious ones would profit. It did not.
  • FailedRicher policies would hurt rules more than prices. No clear difference.
  • FailedA coarse price would be gamed by bunching. It failed another way.
  • FailedThe codebase study was expected to kill the gate idea outright. The gate edged ahead by a margin too small to matter.
  • FailedThe gate's edge would grow with more agents. It did not.
  • In partA price on visible coupling would match the gate when hiding is pointless. True for six agents, not twelve.
