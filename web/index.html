Kant fix it

But a price can.

Can a conscience stop self-improving AI agents from wrecking a shared system, or does it take a price? Five pre-registered studies, 4,190 runs.

See what came out Read the report

Six agents, one system

Each agent keeps whatever helps it. What helps one can quietly hurt the rest.

Everything else was held fixed: the agents, how hard they search, the world they share. Only one thing changed between runs: the rule that decides whether an agent keeps a change. A selfish rule, a Kantian rule ("would I still want this if everyone did it?"), welfare rules, and prices an operator could charge instead.

Two worlds were simulated: an interbank payment network, where delaying payments saves you money and costs everyone else; and a shared codebase, where shortcuts add hidden coupling and the incidents land anywhere.

What came out

The rule matters

In the payment network, selfish agents left the system 29 % costlier than a planner would. The Kantian rule, which only ever looks at the agent's own costs, closed about half of that gap, and the gain carried over to shocks the agents never trained on.

A price matters more

A plain charge on payment delay beat every rule, including the best one, with simple agent policies and with richer, rule-level ones.

Charge what you can see, and they hide it

In the shared codebase, a charge on visible coupling taught the agents to conceal it. 99 % of the coupling went underground and the system cost rose by more than half. A tax on the damage itself, wherever it lands, could not be dodged that way.

The conscience fails when agents are unequal

When some modules absorb most of the incidents, "what if everyone did this?" measures what an agent would suffer, not what it causes. The exposed agents froze, the sheltered ones ran wild, and the shared-damage tax won. A follow-up study showed the result holds whatever scale "the same change" is measured on.

Kant can't. A price can.

How it was kept honest

5

studies, each pre-registered before its runs

4,190

confirmatory runs on held-out scenarios

7 of 29

registered predictions failed, and are reported as failed

82

tests on accounting, determinism and isolation

  • Hypotheses, analysis code and a decision rule were committed before every confirmatory study; the commit hashes are the timestamps.
  • Pilots ran on separate seeds and a separate scenario split. Results come from scenario families never seen during evolution.
  • Rerunning the analysis scripts regenerates every reported number exactly from the stored records.
  • A literature review in three passes checked each claim against prior work, and corrected the ones that did not hold.
