Final report of the gates line, 18 November 2026, corrected 23 November 2026 after an independent review (§4.5, §4.6). Status: single-site, not peer reviewed; an independent reviewer re-ran 120 headline runs and reproduced them exactly, but the results are not independently replicated. The audit trail, decision log and per-study detail are in PROJECT.md. The frozen protocols are PREREGISTRATION.md (E1–E6), PREREGISTRATION_E8.md, PREREGISTRATION_E9.md and PREREGISTRATION_E10.md.

Abstract

Self-improving agent systems share a propose–evaluate–accept loop. When several such loops share an environment, each accepted change alters the conditions under which the others are evaluated, and changes that are good for their owner can be bad for everyone. We ask whether the acceptance rule alone can correct this, and whether it can do so better than a charge on the externality that an operator could levy instead. Proposals, search budget and environment are identical across arms; only the gate, or the price the agents face, differs. Five pre-registered studies and a pre-registered corrected rerun (4 430 confirmatory run records, some of them shared between studies, 20 paired seeds each, held-out evaluation scenarios) were run in two environments.

In a stylized real-time gross settlement network (E1–E6, E8), private gates leave the system 29 % costlier than a symmetric planner. A universalization (Kantian) gate, which reads only the agent's own cost when every agent makes the same change, closes 51 % of that gap. A gate that evaluates joint moves does better still. But a delay surcharge on private agents does better than every gate, with three-parameter and with eight-parameter rule-level policies.

In a shared-codebase environment (E9, E10), harm is unattributable and its carrier can be hidden, so no direct price exists. With identical agents the Kantian gate and a budget-breaking ambient tax are practically equivalent (difference 0.0009, 0.4 % of cost). Every budget-balanced charge we registered either had no effect or was gamed into concealment. When incident exposure is unequal, the ambient tax beats the best gate (by 0.0022 and 0.0060 at two levels of inequality). The gate shows the distortion our derivation predicts: a Kantian point that is Pareto efficient but weights agents by one over their exposure, so it misses the minimum of total cost. Under a decision rule fixed in advance, the claim that gates beat the best available instrument is therefore rejected. A fifth study (E11) re-ran the Kantian gates with "the same change" measured on three other scales. The gate's cost moved by up to 0.02 with the choice of scale, but the ambient tax still beat the best gate over every variant implemented. An independent review later found that the square-root variant was implemented incorrectly and that the scales used different conventions; a corrected, pre-registered rerun (E12) reached the same verdict (§4.5).

Correction (post hoc). Earlier versions concluded that where only budget-balanced charges are possible and the harm's carrier can be hidden, an own-cost Kantian gate beats those charges. An independent review found a budget-balanced rule we had not tested: equal sharing of shipped output. In this model it makes exactly the same decisions as the level-1 ambient tax (identical trajectories, verified), so that conditional role for the gate does not hold here (§4.6). Several registered predictions failed; they are listed in §5.

1. Question

Systems that improve themselves by proposing changes, scoring them and keeping the ones that score well are now common (for example CORAL, Qu et al. 2026; Hyperagents, Zhang et al. 2026; AIDE², Srikanth et al. 2026). They are evaluated as single lineages, or as several agents pursuing one common objective, against fixed benchmarks. In use, several such systems owned by different principals share an environment: banks sharing a payment system, teams sharing a codebase. Then a change that lowers its owner's cost can raise everyone else's, and a loop that accepts on own cost reproduces a social dilemma.

The loop has a natural control point that is neither the proposer nor the environment: the acceptance gate. This work treats the gate as the experimental factor and asks two questions.

  1. Does the choice of gate change the outcome, at matched search budget?
  2. Can a gate do better than the textbook alternative, a price on the externality chosen by an operator?

The second question decides whether gates are worth building. It was answered in two environments chosen to make it progressively harder for the price: one where the externality can be priced directly, and one where it cannot be attributed.

2. Related work

A literature review is in LITERATURE_REVIEW.md. It combines 118 logged web searches with a second pass through the arXiv, Crossref and OpenAlex APIs, and a manual Google Scholar pass. It is a broad structured review, not a formal systematic one, and several sources were seen at abstract level only. This section summarises it.

Acceptance gates. In 2026 the acceptance gate of a self-improving loop became a research object in its own right, but only for single agents. SAGE (arXiv:2609.36043) compares a statistical gate with a greedy one at matched proposals and tokens. PACE (arXiv:2606.08106) compares greedy, fixed-sample, false-discovery-rate and anytime-valid gates. So et al. (2026) compare ten gate rules across 411 loops. SAGE and PACE name the multi-agent case as open. When learning rules themselves are under selection, selfish learning is unstable and other-regarding rules are favoured (McAvoy et al. 2022, two-player repeated games). Single-lineage systems such as the Darwin Gödel Machine and Hyperagents compare archive-based acceptance with keeping only one successor. The closest classical analogue is Agogino and Tumer's comparison of local, global and difference evaluation for evolving multiagent policies. It is fully cooperative, and it shapes fitness rather than accepting or rejecting proposals. We did not find a comparison of per-agent gates among co-evolving agents that impose costs on each other, nor one against a price.

Kantian theory. Roemer (2015) shows that additive and multiplicative Kantian equilibria are Pareto efficient, including with heterogeneous agents. Only the simple form, in which everyone takes the same action level, is generally inefficient with heterogeneity. Homo moralis preferences (Alger and Weibull 2013) give the degree-of-morality mixture κ. Partial Kantian equilibria are defined by Kordonis (2020), through imagined groups of co-movers who minimise their mean cost, and by Istrate (2020). Our κ mixes two scores of the agent's own cost instead. Behind a veil of ignorance, with roles assigned at random, universalization implies utilitarian altruism (Laslier 2022). Our κ = 1 gate is the additive form: every agent's policy moves by the same mutation. With unequal exposure, the additive Kantian condition gives b′ᵢ = n·eᵢ·D′. The equal-weight cost optimum gives b′ᵢ = n·ē·D′. So the Kantian point is the welfare optimum with weights proportional to 1/exposure. That is efficient in Pareto's sense, but not the minimum of total cost. This derivation is ours. It is not stated in the sources we found, and E10 tests its prediction. It is consistent with Laslier's result: E10 fixes each agent's exposure rather than randomising it, which is exactly when universalization and total-cost minimisation separate. Mohanty, Rao and Roy (2024) study Kantian agents in local public-goods networks, in theory. For asymmetric positions they argue that a Kantian duty must depend on the agent's role, and they reject as ad hoc the uniform rule in which everyone mirrors one's own action regardless of position (their §5.1). That uniform rule is our κ = 1 gate; E10 measures its cost inside a learning loop and against a tax, which they do not. Universalization has been used as a decision-time prompt for language-model agents (Piatti et al. 2024). We did not find it used as the acceptance rule of a learning loop.

Payment systems. The payment-timing game is a standard example of privately rational delay with collective cost (Bech and Garratt 2003; Galbiati and Soramäki 2011). Learning agents in it have been studied with reinforcement learning (Castro et al. 2025). A single prompted language-model agent has been tested on cash-management decisions (Aldasoro and Desai 2025), and prompted agents have been made to interact in an intraday-liquidity game (Danisman et al. 2026). Pricing beating coordination rules has precedent: a time-rising intraday tariff beats throughput guidelines in most cases (Ota 2011). Late-settlement fees have also been studied as a coordination device (Nellen 2019). Our surcharge result extends that comparison to agent-level gates. Fu et al. (2026) compare stabilising mechanisms for language-model agents in other financial coordination games; there paid commitments were never the best mechanism. Their agents are prompted, not evolved.

Unattributable harm. With output observed only in aggregate, no budget-balanced sharing rule gives efficient incentives, and a budget breaker can (Holmström 1982). The ambient tax on non-point pollution is the corresponding instrument (Segerson 1988). Known objections include collusion, large transfers and information requirements (Millock and Salanié 2005). Laboratory evidence is mixed (Spraggon 2002; Cochard, Willinger and Xepapadeas 2005; Suter et al. 2008). Spraggon found the tax effective for groups but not for individuals. Cochard et al. studied polluters who bear the damage themselves, as our agents bear incidents. There, subjects under the ambient tax cooperated to maximise group payoff, cut input below the optimum, and ended worse off than without regulation. In another experiment, heterogeneity in farm size and location reduced the effectiveness of an ambient tax/subsidy unless peer information was given; an agent-based model calibrated on that experiment exists (Wu 2017). Charging each agent all damage outside its module makes its payoff equal to private benefit minus total damage, a Groves-style transfer. The level-1 identity between the ambient tax and the unilateral welfare gate is therefore an observation, not a result. In multi-agent reinforcement learning, LOPT (Hua et al. 2025) learns a Pigouvian reward shaping toward social welfare where the externality can be attributed. Danassis et al. (2026) use a learning policymaker to set market prices against common-pool externalities, where harvests are observable. Paying on a measured dimension moves effort into unmeasured ones (Holmström and Milgrom 1991; not re-read for this review).

Technical debt. Gavidia-Calderon et al. (2020) model technical debt as a tragedy of the commons. In their simulation, two developers choose between a kludge and a fix, and the only equilibrium is that both kludge. Code review with enough detection restores fixing. Our codebase environment adds continuous shortcut intensity, concealed coupling, hardening, incidents that rise convexly with debt and land in random modules, operator charges, and many evolving agents. Developers describe AI-assisted coding in the same terms: individual productivity gains push costs onto reviewers and maintainers (Baltes et al. 2026).

Every comparison here therefore builds on known pieces. What we add is a matched-budget comparison of per-agent acceptance gates against prices among co-evolving agents, in two environments. That comparison was not found in this review.

3. Method

3.1 Common protocol

Loop. n agents, 40 generations. In each generation each agent proposes four Gaussian mutations of its policy, scores each on 16 fresh development scenarios, and accepts the best if the gate's score is negative. All agents update together. Every arm spends the same number of simulations.

Gates. Each gate scores a candidate change by:

  • private (κ = 0): own cost under the unilateral change;
  • universalization (κ = 1): own cost when every agent makes the same shift;
  • κ ∈ (0, 1): a mixture of the two;
  • unilateral welfare: total cost under the unilateral change;
  • joint welfare: total cost under the joint shift;
  • no-harm (payments only): the combined cost of all other agents does not rise. An individual agent can lose if the others together gain more.

The own-cost gates read only the agent's own cost; the welfare gates read everyone's.

Prices. These are transfers added to private costs, with private gates. Each price level is chosen on development cost in the last generation and reported on holdout.

Evaluation. Final profiles are scored on 100 held-out scenarios in each of five scenario families, four of them shocks never seen during evolution. The outcome is system cost per unit of value, the equal-weight mean of the five families.

Statistics. Seeds are the unit (20, paired across arms). We report the 95 % percentile bootstrap (10 000 resamples) and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with Holm correction where registered. "Clearly" means the interval excludes 0. E9 and E10 add an equivalence margin of ±0.002.

Integrity.

  • The evolution code cannot import the evaluator or construct holdout scenarios; a test checks this.
  • Cost regimes, budgets and price grids were chosen on separate pilot seeds and a separate scenario split.
  • Each protocol and its analysis script were committed before the confirmatory runs.
  • Records carry the commit hash, and rerunning the analyses reproduces the reported results byte for byte.

3.2 Payment environment (E1–E6, E7, E8)

Setup. Six banks, 20 ticks, integer money and no intraday credit. 85 % of payments lie on closed cycles, so that liquidity serves timing rather than net funding.

Policy. Opening liquidity fraction, buffer fraction and hold time. E8 extends this to eight parameters (two buffers, an opening tick, a large-payment threshold with its own hold time, and a bilateral sending limit), with the original space exactly nested.

Costs. All borne by the sender: liquidity 1, full-day delay 0.5, deadline miss 2, unsettled at close 10.

Prices. A delay surcharge proportional to the settlement tick; E8 adds a coarser two-band tariff. The reference is a symmetric planner.

3.3 Codebase environment (E9, E10)

Policy and debt. Six agents (twelve in a secondary regime) own modules in a shared codebase. Each tunes three numbers:

  • shortcut intensity s, which raises output;
  • concealed share m of the resulting coupling;
  • private hardening q.

Coupling feeds a shared debt stock that decays slowly. Incidents arrive in every module at a rate convex in the debt, and surface wherever they hit. Concealed coupling is invisible to the operator, costs its author a share γ of the shortcut's gain and adds 50 % more debt.

Operator's view. The operator sees visible coupling, output, and where incidents surface. It does not see policies or concealed coupling.

Instruments, all on observables:

  • proxy: a charge on visible coupling;
  • activity: a charge on output;
  • surface: a charge on incident loss in the agent's own module;
  • ambient tax: a charge on incident loss in every other module, levied on each agent in full. This is the budget-breaking Segerson–Holmström form; at level 1 it is identical to the unilateral welfare gate (a tested identity; a Groves-style observation, §2).

Optimum. E9 finds it by exhaustive search over symmetric profiles. E10 uses coordinate descent from there over asymmetric profiles.

Exposure (E10 only). E10 multiplies agent a's incident rate by rank^-skew (mean 1), so central modules absorb most incidents while every module still adds coupling.

  • Universalization closes 51 % of the gap (41–63 %; H2), and cost falls monotonically in κ (H2b).
  • The gain transfers to every held-out shock family (H4).

  • Heterogeneity in bank size did not erode the gain (H3; closure 0.51, 0.39 and 0.52).

Post hoc (E7, not registered). Requiring a paired t-statistic below −2 for acceptance preserves the ordering of gates. It removes the no-harm gate's advantage and makes every arm much worse at this budget.

Corrections (post hoc, independent review). Three problems were found after publication. 4.6 Equal output sharing (post hoc, independent review)

This section was not pre-registered. An independent review of the published repository proposed a budget-balanced rule that none of our studies included: charge each agent its shipped output minus the average shipped output, paying a rebate when that is negative. The charges sum to zero in every scenario, and the rule needs only each module's shipped output, which the operator observes. It is equal sharing of output, a textbook sharing rule (Holmström 1982), and close to reward-transfer schemes studied for learning agents (Willis et al. 2024; Haupt et al. 2024).

  • It is equivalent to the level-1 ambient tax in this model. On the development scenarios output is worth one unit and module sizes do not depend on policy. An agent's pooled cost then changes by exactly one nth of the total cost change when it alone changes policy, while under the ambient tax it changes by the whole total. Every gate decision depends only on the sign of that score and on a scale-free t-test, so the two rules make the same decisions.
  • Verified, not only derived. The reviewer's harness and our own test (test_pooling_reproduces_the_ambient_tax_trajectory) show identical policies, histories and simulation counts at skews 0, 0.75 and 1.5; the transfers balance to within 10⁻¹³.
  • What it changes. C5b and D5 hold only against the budget-balanced charges we registered. With equal output sharing available, a budget-balanced rule does as well as the best instrument at every skew, so this model gives the gate no role. The headline is unchanged: a transfer, not a conscience, reaches the optimum, and now even without breaking the budget.
  • Why it works here, and where it would not. The simulated agents have no costs or benefits outside the shared ledger. If agents bore private costs, such as effort or compute, equal sharing would give each only one nth of the gain from bearing them, the team problem Holmström identified, while the ambient tax would not. Whether a gate helps in that setting is open and would need its own registered study.

6. Discussion

What the gate does. In the payment game, gains appear only when several banks shift together, so a unilateral probe of a good joint direction looks bad. The universalization gate works mainly by evaluating coordinated moves; the joint-welfare gate does the same with more information. In the codebase environment with identical agents, the Kantian gate is efficient, as Roemer's theory predicts.

Why a price wins where one exists. In payments the externality runs through settlement timing, and a charge on settlement time prices that channel directly. It needs no change to the agents and no counterfactual simulation of others. Richer policies did not erode it, but a coarse price is fragile: richer policies exploit what it leaves unpriced.

Why the ambient tax wins where no direct price exists. The ambient tax charges each agent what it imposes on others, whoever suffers it. The Kantian question "what if everyone did this?" equals that harm only when everyone is equally exposed. With unequal exposure it measures what the agent would suffer. Central agents then over-restrain and peripheral agents over-shortcut. The additive Kantian point is still Pareto efficient: it is the optimum for a planner who weights each agent by one over its exposure (§2). It is not the minimum of total cost, which is the outcome measured here. Mohanty et al. (2024) anticipate the general point from theory: with unequal positions a uniform Kantian rule is not the right duty. The 1/exposure derivation is ours, not taken from the literature, and it is for a scalar game with separable gains and shared aggregate damage. The simulated codebase has hardening, output-dependent losses, bounded choices and noisy finite search, so E10's pattern of shortcuts is consistent with the derivation; it does not show that the learned profiles are Pareto efficient in the simulated game.

What survives. Earlier versions said that where an operator can neither price the carrier of harm nor break the budget, every budget-balanced charge fails and the gate is the best option. That was wrong for this model: equal output sharing is budget-balanced, needs only shipped output, and does as well as the ambient tax (§4.6). The comparison set was incomplete, against our own rule to include the strongest instrument an operator could realistically use. The gate therefore has no role in these models. A role could exist where agents bear private costs, where risk aversion or collusion matter, or where transfers are capped; these studies hold all of them fixed.

What each rule needs to observe. The rules differ in information, and the matched search budget does not equalise that. Equal output sharing needs each module's shipped output. The ambient tax needs the incident losses that land on others. The Kantian gate needs no measurement by an operator but must simulate a world in which every other agent makes its change, so it needs a model of the others' policies. A fair statement of which rule is realistic depends on which of these an operator can obtain.

7. Limitations

  • Toy models.
  • Payments: six banks, uncalibrated costs, no liquidity-saving mechanism, and 3–7 % of value left unsettled. The dilemma exists only under balanced flows.
  • Codebase: one functional form, an incident process calibrated on pilot data to produce a dilemma, and one kind of heterogeneity.
  • Weak search. Mutation proposals over three or eight bounded numbers. Acceptance is noise-dominated (about five of six proposals accepted per generation in payments). Every arm degrades with richer policies at fixed budget.
  • Imperfect references.
  • The payment planner is symmetric, so gap closure there is understated.
  • The codebase optimum is a grid and coordinate-descent optimum, possibly local, and the tuned ambient tax beats it (0.2136 against 0.2138 in E9 at level 1.5; 0.1747 against 0.1749 at skew 1.5). Every "share of the gap" and "best possible" figure is measured against this reference, not a true optimum.
  • Favourable assumptions for the ambient tax. Agents are risk-neutral and cannot collude, which removes the classic objections to it. Our agents each accept changes by their own gate and cannot coordinate. Human subjects who bore the damage themselves coordinated under an ambient tax and over-restrained (Cochard et al. 2005). Heterogeneity also reduced the tax's effectiveness among humans (Wu 2017). So E9 and E10's results for the tax hold for these agents, not necessarily for people.
  • The Kantian gate depends on units. The Kantian counterfactual depends on how actions are represented (Sloev and Lianos 2026). E11 and its corrected rerun E12 confirm this: the gate's cost moved by up to 0.02 across the scales tested, and the verdict held under all of them. Other scales were not tested.
  • Many tests, no multiplicity correction. Each study registered a family of tests with no adjustment, and several verdicts lie close to the 0.002 practical margin. Those should be read as indicative.
  • E8 provenance. E8's protocol was frozen at 9729748, but its runs carry the "dirty" flag: the analysis script was written while they started and was committed with the results (0547687). The simulation code did not change (PROJECT.md §5.8).
  • No language models. Proposals are random mutations, so the link to self-improving language-model systems is an analogy, not a result.
  • Prior-art review was broad and structured but not a formal systematic review; several sources were seen at abstract level only (LITERATURE_REVIEW.md). It missed work on reward transfers between learning agents (Willis et al. 2024; Haupt et al. 2024), which bears directly on §4.6, and on Kantian behaviour in public-goods networks (Mohanty et al. 2024), which anticipates the unequal-exposure mechanism conceptually (§2); it was read in full after the review.

8. Conclusion

Across two environments and five pre-registered studies, the acceptance gate is a consequential design choice, but it is not a substitute for a well-chosen price. A price on the externality matches or beats every gate tested:

  • a delay surcharge where the channel is observable;
  • an ambient tax where it is not.

In these models a budget-balanced transfer, equal sharing of output, does as well as the ambient tax (§4.6, post hoc), so we found no setting in which the gate is needed. That equivalence rests on agents having no private costs outside the shared ledger.

The line is closed. Four things could reopen it, none of them in scope here:

  • agents with private costs, where equal sharing under-incentivises;
  • risk-averse agents facing a noisy ambient charge;
  • collusion against the ambient tax;
  • open-ended action spaces, such as agents that edit real code under a sandbox. There a stronger proposer might find exploits of prices that mutation search cannot.

Reproducibility

Code, raw records and analysis scripts are in this repository; commands are in PROJECT.md §8. The confirmatory runs take about four hours on a laptop. Results are deterministic given the commit, and the analysis scripts regenerate the result files exactly from the stored records.
